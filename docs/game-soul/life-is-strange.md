# Life is Strange — Heart and Soul
- Developer / studio: Dontnod Entertainment (co-directors Michel Koch and Raoul Barbet; writer Jean-Luc Cano; producer Luc Baghadoust); published by Square Enix
- Genre / platforms: Episodic narrative adventure; PS4/PS3, Xbox One/X360, PC (2015)
- Why players come back (in the devs' view): A single impossible choice, entirely about the player's own feelings. Writer Jean-Luc Cano: "We wanted it to be all about your own feelings — for there to be no right or wrong decision. More than that, this choice is really a metaphor for growing older." Co-director Michel Koch: "The whole game builds up to literally that moment. The point of the game is not to give that linear conclusion. It's a choose-your-own adventure book... in the end, Max has to take one of those two decisions. We're really happy with those endings, because it really conveys for us that main message: you cannot try to cheat everything... there's always some compromise." Refusing a canon ending (Christian Divine: "People can kind of create their own version of what happened") kept the debate alive for a decade and protected players' ownership: declaring one canon would tell players "Hey cool, you made a choice. That choice was wrong."

I would feel bad because what's so gut wrenching about the game is the choice at the end." — Philip Bache, voice director, USgamer/PAX West 2018 (via VG247), 2018 [unverified]
- Why players love/support it: Max-and-Chloe's relationship (romantic or not) is the heart; the near-50/50 ending split proves both sides feel true. Players come back to debate the ending, re-play to sit with different choices, and the community is still discussing it 10+ years later. Deck Nine's 2025 "Life is Strange: Reunion" markets itself as giving the pair "the ending they deserve" — the devotion outlived Dontnod's own tenure.
- Monetization philosophy (devs' words, if any): Episodic model (bought per episode, then season). No on-record dev monetization philosophy found.
- Lesson for our games (1-2 lines, concrete): A finale with *no right answer* that forces the player to live with a trade-off generates a decade of community debate — design endings around the player's own feelings, not puzzle-box logic. And never invalidate a player's ending by canonizing one.
