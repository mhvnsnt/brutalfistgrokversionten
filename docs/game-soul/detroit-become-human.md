# Detroit: Become Human — Heart and Soul
- Developer / studio: Quantic Dream (founder/director David Cage; head writer Adam Williams); published by Sony Interactive Entertainment
- Genre / platforms: Interactive drama / branching narrative adventure; PS4 (2018), PC (2019)
- Why players come back (in the devs' view): Real choice with real consequences — and *showing* players what they missed. Cage: "What's interesting on Detroit is that we managed to make people replay, so they could see all of the different branches — which is quite rare in a narrative game. We achieved this because we showed all of the branches and the variations of the story." Hiding branches in Heavy Rain/Beyond was "not a good decision"; the flowchart was "a better compromise, because it was about showing part of what you missed, and that played a major role in the success of the game." The design ethic: "no smoke and mirrors" — produce whole scenes that only ~10–20% of players will see, "because that 20% will talk about it with someone else. They will compare their stories and realize they are totally different." All three protagonists can die permanently and "the game will carry on" — players get "a narrative fingerprint" where "no two people are going to get quite the same experience." The goal: "give the feeling to the player that they are in control of their destiny, that they are telling their own story."

  - "What's interesting on Detroit is that we managed to make people replay, so they could see all of the different branches — which is quite rare in a narrative game. We achieved this because we showed all of the branches and the variations of the story." — David Cage, director, Gamelab Barcelona (via PlayStation LifeStyle), 2019 [unverified]
  - "It was important for me not to use smoke and mirrors, because in this genre it is very easy to do it and say this choice has tremendous consequences but really it doesn't. It had a cost in production to say 'Okay, we're going to produce scenes that maybe 20% of the players will see in their first playthrough, but let's do it, because that 20% will talk about it with someone else. They will compare their stories and realize they are totally different.'" — David Cage, director, Official PlayStation Magazine UK (via Wccftech), 2017 [unverified]
  - "The story you get when you play the game will be incredibly personal to you. You're going to get a kind of 'narrative fingerprint,' because no two people are going to get quite the same experience... There's not a cannon [canon] version of Detroit. It's not a single story you can tweak, it's a context in which you can make your own story." — Adam Williams, head writer, IGN First (via Wccftech), 2018 [unverified]
  - "The idea with Detroit is really to create a game where the player is not only the actor, but also the core writer of the story. We really want players to be able to tell their own story in this world and make choices, make decisions that will really shape the narrative and make each story unique." — David Cage, director, Gamereactor, 2017 [unverified]
- Why players love/support it: Players compare flowchart run-throughs — the social layer of "what did YOU do" — and replay to see unseen branches. Millions of Twitch/YouTube playthroughs turned each player's unique story into shareable content.
- Monetization philosophy (devs' words, if any): Premium single-purchase; no on-record dev monetization philosophy found.
- Lesson for our games (1-2 lines, concrete): Show players what they missed — a visible flowchart of unchosen paths turns one playthrough into a replay hook. And pay the production cost for scenes only a minority will see; that 20% becomes your evangelists.
